Does parking sale proceeds in Treasuries for 24–36 months soften crash-entry risk?

The question behind a phased-investing plan is not “does it feel safer?” It is: over every historical start date we can observe, did the phased path actually end with more purchasing power than investing immediately? This page replays both strategies on 1928–2025 S&P 500 total returns (dividends included), 3-month Treasury bill returns and CPI-U inflation, using one shared start date, one shared end date and an identical starting amount of 1.00. Multiply any multiple below by private sale proceeds to see an illustrative outcome — the site stores no such figure.

!
Headline result: phasing usually costs money, and only pays in a genuine crash.

Across 89 overlapping ten-year windows, the 24-month park plus 36-month phase-in beat immediate investing in only 15.7% of them. The median ten-year real outcome was −74.9 pts versus lump sum. The wins are real but concentrated: the best window started in 1929 (+107.9 pts), the worst started in 1949 (−307.1 pts).

1 · What this test does and does not prove

Compared

Lump sum invests everything in month 1. Phased parks everything in 3-month T-bills for 24 months, then buys equities in 36 equal monthly tranches, so it is fully invested after 60 months.

Measured in

Real (inflation-adjusted) multiples of the starting amount. Nominal gains during the 1970s are not counted as wins.

Not modelled

Taxes, fees, advisory costs, asset allocation, rebalancing, or the behaviour of any specific account. Both paths hold the same assets once fully invested.

Known limitation

Annual data is smoothed into monthly steps. A crash that happens mid-year is understated, which biases results against phasing in exactly the cases phasing is meant to help.

3 · The same question under three different parking rules

If the phased result were an artefact of assuming T-bills pay something, it would flip when cash earns nothing. It does flip the other way: even crediting uninvested cash with the equity return — impossible in practice — phasing still trails on the median. The conclusion is driven by time out of the market, not by the cash yield assumption.

7 · How to use this in the decision

  1. Do not treat phasing as free risk reduction. It buys crash protection with a measurable median cost and a 60-month delay to full exposure.
  2. Pre-commit the rule and the trigger. If phasing is chosen, write down the tranche schedule, the acceleration trigger, and the date the plan ends — before any sale closes.
  3. Size it to what a crash would actually cost you. Phasing matters most when the sale proceeds are large relative to the rest of your wealth and when a drawdown would force a change of plan.
  4. Keep the reserve separate. Near-term cash needs — taxes, repairs, insurance, estate liquidity — belong in the Treasury ladder regardless of the equity decision.
  5. Re-run annually and after any law change. Extend the dataset each year, re-run python3 scripts/build_blackswan.py, and record the new result in the decision journal.
