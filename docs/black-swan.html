Stress Testing

Black Swan Conditions & Phased Investing

Growth and inflation are not consistent over 50 years. This section stress-tests sell/reinvest paths against 1929/2008-style crash analogs and a stagflation analog, and compares a phased Treasury-to-stock deployment path.

Illustrative only. These are scenario analogs, not forecasts. Historical analogs do not repeat exactly. Use them to understand sequence risk, reinvestment risk, inflation risk, and liquidity risk.

Phased investing path (required test)

Path tested: After inheritance/sale, park proceeds in 3-month to 2-year U.S. Treasuries for 24 months, then phase into diversified stocks over 36 months.

What it can soften

  • Crash-entry sequence risk: reduces chance that the full lump sum hits equities immediately before a 1929/2008-style drawdown.
  • Behavioral panic risk: a written phased plan can reduce impulsive all-in/all-out decisions.
  • Liquidity optionality: Treasury ladder keeps near-term liquidity while plan executes.

What it does NOT eliminate

  • Reinvestment risk: yields may fall when Treasuries mature; future equity entry prices may be higher.
  • Cash drag / inflation risk: after-tax Treasury yield may trail inflation, especially in stagflation.
  • Market risk after phasing: once invested, the portfolio still faces equity drawdowns.
  • Tax drag: Treasury interest is taxable; realized gains/dividends later taxable.
Headline + reinvestment risk (required): Phased investing may reduce crash-entry sequence risk, but it creates reinvestment and cash-drag risk. Any headline benefit must be shown next to that tradeoff.

Key takeaway: No single deployment rule wins in all regimes. The phased path is a risk-management choice, not a free lunch. It should be chosen only if its tradeoffs match the pre-committed decision criteria.

Baseline real-world conditions (not smooth lines)

  • Inflation is variable: plan with low/base/high inflation bands and stress stagflation; do not assume a constant 2–3% every year.
  • Equity returns are lumpy: sequence-of-returns matters most around large lump-sum events (inheritance, sale, retirement).
  • Housing is cyclical and local: Property A/B values can diverge from national averages; liquidity can dry up during downturns.
  • Rates/taxes change: Treasury yields, CA/federal tax law, property tax rules, and insurance costs can change the ranking.
  • Costs are not optional: maintenance, insurance, vacancy, capex, and taxes occur even in bad years.
